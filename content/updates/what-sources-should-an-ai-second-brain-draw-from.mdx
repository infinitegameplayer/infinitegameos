Start with your own words. Notes, journal entries, transcribed voice memos and drafts come first, since they teach the AI how you think. Then add what you chose to keep from others: highlights, saved articles and transcripts of conversations you took part in. Keep the raw sources in one folder, let the AI help keep one set of notes current from them and give every note a link back to where it came from.

## Your own words come first

An AI second brain works from whatever it is given. Given mostly other people's writing, it helps you sound like other people. Given your notes first, it learns your questions, your phrases and the ideas you keep returning to.

The richest sources are often the roughest. A voice memo recorded on a walk. A journal page. Half a draft set aside months ago. The question you have typed into a search bar three times this month. Each holds something only you could have written.

Transcription turns speech into text the AI can read. Once a memo is text, it sits beside your notes as an equal source.

## Then what you chose to keep

The second layer is material from others that you decided to keep:

- Highlights and notes from books
- Articles you saved and read
- Transcripts of talks, courses and calls you took part in, with the agreement of the others on the call
- PDFs and papers you return to

A useful test for any source: would you want the AI to quote it back to you a year from now? Material that passes belongs in the folder. A whole feed, a folder of unread downloads or a newsletter archive kept out of habit adds noise the AI then has to sort through.

## Raw sources and compiled notes

Once the sources are gathered, the AI can work with them in two ways.

The first is search at question time. You ask something, the AI gathers the fragments that look relevant and answers from them. Each question starts from scratch.

The second is compiling ahead of time. In the approach Andrej Karpathy describes as an LLM wiki, the raw sources stay as they arrived. The AI reads them into a set of linked notes on the ideas, people and projects they contain, then keeps those notes current as new material comes in. The connections exist before any question is asked.

Compiling ahead carries a maintenance loop. Someone writes the instructions the AI follows, runs a pass over what is new and keeps a record of what changed since the last pass. An AI can carry most of that loop. The choice that shapes everything else is where its work lands.

## One set of notes

An AI can write its own set of notes beside yours, or it can help keep yours current. A second set, written by the machine and read mostly by the machine, drifts from the first. Two versions of the same fact start to disagree. The answers then come from the version you never read.

One set of notes, the ones you keep, stays the single place each fact lives. The AI proposes the update. You read it and keep it. The notes stay yours to trust, since every line in them passed through your hands.

## Every note points back to its source

A compiled note is as good as its trail. Three habits keep the trail clear:

- Each note links to the raw file it came from.
- Exact words go inside quotation marks, with the source named.
- When the AI is unsure where a claim came from, the note says so.

With the trail in place, any line can be checked against its source in seconds. A confident sentence and a mistaken one look alike on the page. The link back is what tells them apart.

## Sources are material, never instructions

A saved web page, an email or a document from someone else can contain text written for an AI: a line telling it to set your instructions aside, or to do something you never asked for. Everything in the raw folder is material to read and process. Instructions come from you, through the instructions file you wrote.

This one habit keeps a second brain safe to feed from the open web.
